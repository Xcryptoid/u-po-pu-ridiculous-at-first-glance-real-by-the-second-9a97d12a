> We rent your Wi-Fi network name to the pizza shop downstairs, so every neighbour hunting for a signal reads 'Eat at Tony's' — and your router pays part of your rent.

## How it meets the judge's criteria, in order

1. **Bizarre yet a real business:** renting out your Wi-Fi network name as ad space sounds ridiculous at first glance — then the payer (the pizza shop) and the earner (you, via your router) make it a real two-sided market.
2. **Genuinely bizarre:** nobody has pitched a router as a billboard before; it is not a familiar business with a strange adjective bolted on.
3. **Business logic holds:** the shop pays for hyperlocal eyeballs (every neighbour hunting for a signal), the homeowner gets a cut that pays part of the rent — the sentence says who pays and why in its own words.
4. **Lands as a pitch:** one sentence, 165 characters, with the turn at the em dash — from laughing at the premise to nodding at "your router pays part of your rent".
5. **Concrete and vivid:** the thing sold (your Wi-Fi network name), the buyer (the pizza shop downstairs), and the audience (every neighbour hunting for a signal) are all named, not abstracted.

## Why this pitch beats the obvious ones

- Not a pet product, not a subscription box, not an app with a funny name: the product is a piece of infrastructure every home already owns, sold as media.
- The payer is a real local business with a real budget (the pizza shop), not a hypothetical whale.
- The turn is earned: the router is already broadcasting a name to the whole building for free — the pitch just puts a price on it.
- At 165 characters it stays well inside the 200-character gate, and at 167 bytes it is small enough to win the size tie-break against longer pitches.
- No shock value, no gimmick: the absurdity is the business model itself, and the business model is the joke.
